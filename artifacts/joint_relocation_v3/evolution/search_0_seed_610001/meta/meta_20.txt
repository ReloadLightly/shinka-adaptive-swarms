# GLOBAL INSIGHTS SCRATCHPAD

The following are global insights about optimization approaches and their effectiveness:

## Successful Algorithmic Patterns
- **Generation 17, `constant_radius_11875`, is the new best**, superseding Generation 8. Keeping `count=min(4, int(swarm_size))` and reducing the constant radius multiplier from **1.25 to 1.1875** raises the exact score from **0.210153 to 0.210589**, reducing mean error from **3.7584 to 3.7486**—a **0.26% improvement**.
- **Four-particle constant policies remain the strongest observed pattern.** Generation 5’s radius **1.5** achieved **0.207810 / 3.8121**; Generations 8/15’s **1.25** achieved **0.210153 / 3.7584**; Generation 17’s **1.1875** leads. Its mean error is **9.88% below** the corrected baseline’s **4.1596** on these search cases.
- **The latest radius reduction also improves aggregate tail diagnostics.** Against Generations 8/15, Generation 17 reduces worst-case error **12.1501 → 11.7499** and case-error standard deviation **2.5965 → 2.5126**, improvements of **3.29%** and **3.23%**, respectively.
## Ineffective Approaches
- **Recovery-conditioned contraction does not improve either leading constant.** Generations 16/18 score **0.209951**, with mean error **3.7630**, versus Generation 17’s **3.7486**. Their contraction activates on approximately **2.23% of responses**, leaving behavior predominantly at radius **1.25**; the results provide no demonstrated benefit from this recovery trigger.
- **Reducing allocation after mild fitness loss worsens performance.** Generation 19’s threshold rule—three particles when relative drop is **≤0.025**, otherwise four—scores **0.194788 / 4.1338**, with **10.28% higher mean error** than Generation 17. It also underperforms the earlier fixed three-particle Generations 11/13 (**0.199622 / 4.0095**). Valid allocations rule out rounding failure as the explanation.
- **Earlier radius expansion results remain unfavorable and nonmonotonic.** Generation 10’s constant **1.375** produces mean error **4.1035**, worse than both **1.25** and **1.5**. State-dependent Generations 12 and 14 likewise remain behind the current best at **3.9041** and **3.9619**. These outcomes establish policy rankings, without identifying why particular expansions hurt.
## Implementation Insights
- **The best program uses a constant action pair, without observed-state adaptation.** It relocates four of five particles at absolute radius **0.59375** or **1.78125**, depending on the supplied default radius. Its successful change is the constant multiplier; it does not use fitness loss, recovery, displacement, or remaining budget.
- **Allocation encoding remains distinct from execution.** Adapter fraction **0.7** encodes four particles, giving an actual allocation fraction of **0.8**. Generation 17’s requested, allocated, and evaluated relocation counts match throughout, with **zero horizon-truncated responses**.
- **Shared simulator mechanisms cannot explain an evolutionary improvement.** All personal memories are reevaluated, velocities are retained, and the fifth particle still makes ordinary PSO moves and consumes evaluations. Generation 17’s detection overhead is **15.31–16.09%**, with memory refresh adding **0.32–1.82%**.
- **Repeated policies are not independent discoveries.** Generation 15 reproduces Generation 8’s behavior and exact metrics; Generations 16/18 implement equivalent recovery-conditioned policies and also yield identical metrics.
## Performance Analysis
- **Rounded scores conceal the new ranking:** Generation 17 **0.210589** > Generations 8/15 **0.210153** > Generations 16/18 **0.209951** > Generation 19 **0.194788**. Because the score is `1/(1+mean_error)`, tail improvements receive no separate reward.
- **Generation 17’s narrow mean advantage is uneven across cases.** It improves **6/16 cases** against Generation 8 and worsens ten. Large reductions in case_003 (**−1.6951**) and case_015 (**−0.4002**) offset regressions elsewhere. Excluding case_015 reverses their mean ranking: **3.2152** for Generation 17 versus **3.1990** for Generation 8.
- **Case_015 remains the principal tracking weakness.** It contributes **19.59%** of Generation 17’s summed case error. Its interval-end error falls from approximately **10.84 to 9.76**, a **9.90% reduction**, but substantial error persists until the next change; the diagnostics do not establish its cause.
- **Best mean performance does not imply best tail performance or generalization.** Earlier three-particle policies retain lower worst-case errors, including **9.05** for Generations 11/13 and **8.21** at radius **1.5**, despite worse means. All five new programs pass validation across 16 cases; this establishes execution correctness and observed search performance, not superiority on fresh cases.

# META RECOMMENDATIONS

The following are actionable recommendations for the next program generations:

1. **Refine the four-particle constant around 1.1875.** Keep `count=min(4, int(observation["swarm_size"]))` and test constant multipliers `1.15625`, `1.21875`, and `1.125` as separate mutations. The latest contraction improved both mean and tail error, but its uneven case gains favor testing both sides of the incumbent rather than assuming further contraction will help.
2. **Switch narrowly between the two leading constants using observed displacement.** Keep four particles; compute `q=max(0, observed_best_displacement)/max(default_radius, 1e-12)` and return `1.25` when `q>T`, otherwise `1.1875`, testing `T=1` and `T=2` separately. These constants have complementary case outcomes; this tests whether public displacement can select between them, without assuming that it predicts the better action.
3. **Try a bounded radius adjustment based on swarm spread.** Keep four particles and compute `s=max(0, swarm_diameter)/max(default_radius, 1e-12)`, then use `radius_scale=1.1875 + 0.03125*max(-1, min(1, s-1))`. This confines adaptation to `[1.15625, 1.21875]` around the successful constant and tests whether compact swarms benefit from slightly tighter relocation while dispersed swarms benefit from slightly broader relocation; that relationship remains a hypothesis.
4. **Retune the strongest three-particle branch independently.** Set `count=min(3, int(observation["swarm_size"]))` and test constant multipliers `1.4375` and `1.5625` separately. Three particles at `1.5` achieved the lowest reported worst-case error, so nearby radii could improve its mean while preserving useful tail behavior; the four-particle preference for smaller radii need not transfer.
5. **Test a geometry-conditioned switch between successful count–radius pairs.** Default to `(count, radius_scale)=(4, 1.1875)`, but choose `(3, 1.5)` when `swarm_diameter/max(default_radius, 1e-12)>T` and `max(0, observed_best_displacement)/max(default_radius, 1e-12)<=1`, testing `T=2` and `T=4` separately and capping counts by swarm size. This combines the strongest mean policy with the strongest reported tail policy, testing whether an already dispersed swarm with limited observed displacement benefits from retaining a second ordinary PSO mover. Unlike the unsuccessful mild-loss rule, the trigger uses geometry, although its predictive value is unproven.
