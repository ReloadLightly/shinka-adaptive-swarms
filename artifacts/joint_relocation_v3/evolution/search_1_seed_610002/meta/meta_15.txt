# GLOBAL INSIGHTS SCRATCHPAD

The following are global insights about optimization approaches and their effectiveness:

## Successful Algorithmic Patterns
- **Generation 11, Spread-Adaptive Partial Relocation, is the new best:** score **0.214212**, mean offline error **3.6683**. Selecting three particles when `swarm_diameter/default_radius > 4`, otherwise four, at radius multiplier **1.25**, reduces mean error **2.40%** versus the previous best, G9 (**0.210153**, **3.7584**), and **11.81%** versus the corrected full-swarm baseline, G0 (**4.1596**).
- **Dispersion-conditioned allocation improves on both tested constant allocations at the same radius.** G11 beats G9’s fixed four particles and G14’s fixed three particles (**0.199622**, error **4.0095**); its error is **8.51% lower** than G14’s. G12’s spread threshold of eight also beats G9, scoring **0.212042** with error **3.7161**. These results update the earlier finding that the strongest evaluated policy was constant, while leaving the independent value of state dependence unestablished.
- **Substantial partial relocation remains the strongest observed pattern.** G11 lowers error **8.71%** versus G5’s full allocation at the same **1.25** radius multiplier. Its advantage combines predominantly four-particle relocation with occasional three-particle responses; neither uniformly reducing allocation nor expanding radius explains the ranking alone.
## Ineffective Approaches
- **Fitness-loss switching continues to underperform constant allocation.** G10’s 10% threshold at radius **1.0** scores **0.198339**, with error **4.0419**, trailing both fixed-three G1 (**3.8942**) and fixed-four G2 (**3.7869**). It also trails the earlier 5% threshold programs G6/G7 (**3.9587**). Increasing the threshold supplied no aggregate advantage; all passed validation, so this is a performance limitation rather than an execution failure.
- **Conditional radius expansion weakens the fixed-four policy.** G13 raises the multiplier from **1.25 to 1.5** when fitness loss exceeds 10% and diameter is below twice the default radius. Its score falls to **0.205086**, with error **3.8760**, versus G9’s **0.210153 / 3.7584**. Worst-case error also worsens from **12.1501 to 12.3022**; the feedback does not establish why.
- **Radius expansion is allocation-dependent.** Earlier improvements from multiplier **1.25** for full and four-particle allocation do not extend to fixed-three allocation: G14’s error **4.0095** is **2.96% higher** than G1’s **3.8942** at multiplier **1.0**. Together with G3’s poor full-swarm multiplier **2.0** result (**0.191518**), this contradicts a generally beneficial radius-expansion effect.
- **Raising the spread threshold reduces the winning policy’s advantage.** G12 changes G11’s threshold from four to eight while retaining radius **1.25**; mean error rises from **3.6683 to 3.7161**, despite identical worst-case error **9.4230**. G11 wins five paired cases, loses four and ties seven, indicating a modest, uneven threshold effect.
## Implementation Insights
- **G11’s distinguishing mechanism is normalized spread controlling allocation alone.** The denominator is floored at `1e-12`, and the requested count is clamped to `[0, swarm_size]`. Radius remains **1.25**, giving absolute radii **0.625** and **1.875** in the two severity settings. Relative to G9, the policy change is the conditional reduction from four particles to three.
- **The winning policy mostly preserves the previous best’s action.** Across **3,440 responses**, G11 allocates four particles **2,951 times (85.78%)** and three **489 times (14.22%)**. Response-weighted allocation is **77.16%** of the five-particle swarm. Requested, allocated and objectively queried relocation counts match, with no horizon truncation.
- **Adapter encoding must remain separate from actual allocation.** Three and four particles correspond to actual fractions **0.6 and 0.8**, whereas adapter values **0.5 and 0.7** encode those integers for the ceiling rule. Non-relocated particles continue ordinary PSO motion; partial relocation does not directly eliminate their objective evaluations.
- **Shared simulator corrections are not evolved improvements.** Memory reevaluation and retained velocities remain fixed across these comparisons. G11 spends **15.32–16.11%** of evaluations on detection and **0.33–1.75%** on memory refresh. These costs are included in performance accounting but do not explain its advantage. The module’s introductory full-allocation baseline description is stale relative to its executable policy.
## Performance Analysis
- **The updated score ordering is:** **G11 > G12 > G9 > G2 > G13 > G1 > G6 = G7 > G8 > G14 > G5 > G10 > G4 > G0 > G3**. Rounding scores to **0.20–0.21** obscures these differences. Because score is `1/(1+mean_error)`, worst-case error and dispersion receive no independent selection weight; G6/G7 remain duplicate behavior, not independent replication.
- **G11’s advantage over G9 is concentrated in case_015.** It improves seven cases and worsens nine. Case_015 error falls **12.1501 → 9.4230**, a reduction of **2.7271**, exceeding the total paired error reduction across all 16 cases (**1.4426**). Excluding that case, G11’s mean is **3.2846**, versus G9’s **3.1990**. Interval-end error improves approximately **10.84 → 7.24**, but substantial tracking difficulty remains.
- **Regime effects are mixed.** Compared with G9, G11 improves mean error at interval **5,000** for severity one (**1.9904 → 1.5904**) and severity three (**5.2494 → 5.0228**). At interval **2,500**, error worsens for severity one (**2.9131 → 3.1192**) and severity three (**4.8808 → 4.9406**). The aggregate gain therefore does not represent uniform improvement.
- **The best mean is not the best tail result.** G11 improves G9’s worst-case error and case-error standard deviation from **12.1501 / 2.5965** to **9.4230 / 2.0817**. G14 achieves still lower values, **9.0543 / 1.9653**, despite substantially worse mean error. All programs pass validation, but these reused 16 search cases establish neither generalization nor a causal benefit from conditioning actions on current spread.

# META RECOMMENDATIONS

The following are actionable recommendations for the next program generations:

1. **Refine G11’s spread threshold locally.** Keep `q = swarm_diameter / max(default_radius, 1e-12)` and radius multiplier `1.25`, but test `count = 3 if q > T else 4` with `T=3`, `5`, and `6` in separate mutations, retaining the existing count clamp. G11’s threshold four outperformed threshold eight, supporting a closer search without assuming that lowering the threshold always helps.
2. **Reduce radius specifically for three-particle responses.** Preserve G11’s allocation rule and use `radius_scale=1.0` or `1.125` when `q>4`, keeping `1.25` otherwise. Fixed-three allocation performed better at radius `1.0` than `1.25`, making this a promising allocation–radius interaction to test, although that result may not transfer to dispersed swarms.
3. **Tune the dominant four-particle branch independently.** Keep three-particle responses at radius `1.25`, and separately try `1.125` and `1.375` for four-particle responses. That branch accounts for approximately 86% of G11’s responses, so modest radius improvements there could matter substantially; the mixed radius results favor small adjustments.
4. **Restrict reduced allocation to a bounded spread range.** At radius `1.25`, try `count = 3 if 4 < q <= U else 4`, with `U=8` and `12` as separate mutations. G11 supports occasional allocation reduction, but its advantage does not establish that three particles remain preferable at extreme dispersion; this variation tests whether very dispersed swarms benefit from restoring four relocations.
5. **Condition the spread threshold on observed attractor displacement.** Define `u=max(0.0, observed_best_displacement)/max(default_radius, 1e-12)` and `T=4.0 + 2.0*u/(1.0+u)`, then choose three particles when `q>T`, otherwise four, retaining radius `1.25`. This extends G11’s successful spatial rule by requiring greater dispersion before reducing allocation when the observed attractor has moved farther; displacement is only a proxy, and the proposed benefit remains untested.
