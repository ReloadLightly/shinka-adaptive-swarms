# Agent B research memo: collective systems, emergence and the cost of maintaining cooperation

**Scope:** `paper_trajectory_v2`; read-only research during the authorized continuation from proposal30, subsequently limited to proposal35. The findings below use generations 5, 12 and 26 and the preserved generation-30 review. They do not describe proposals that had not completed when inspected. No simulations, evaluator invocations, campaign mutations, or additional research-model CLI calls were made. A small extraction of four existing trajectories, with verified compressed-file hashes, is saved in [actor34-evidence.json](actor34-evidence.json) for integration with this memo. Literature research did not enter the frozen discovery context.

**Main finding:** the experiment evolves a common coordination protocol, then lets actors implement it through decentralized bilateral interactions. Some collective outcomes are consequences of those interactions, including construction delays, unequal gains and the collapse of an initially completed cooperative group. The cohort blueprint, public-clock phases and uniform policy adoption are explicit coordination resources. They should not themselves be presented as spontaneously emerging institutions.

## 1. What evolves, what adapts, and what can be called emergent

**Observed implementation.** `java-paper/agents/PaperRunner.java:133–136` loads one candidate class and supplies its constructor to the simulator. `java-paper/agents/PaperSimulation.java:36–45` creates one instance of that same class for every actor and assigns each actor a separate `Memory`. The policy code does not evolve during a trajectory. Actors can change their private state and ties; external Shinka search changes the common policy between evaluations. Its islands hold alternative complete policies, not independently evolving actor species that are jointly assembled into a team.

This matters for terminology. Potter and De Jong's cooperative coevolution evolves interacting, separately represented subcomponents whose fitness depends on their collaborators. The present system evaluates a complete shared policy deployed across all actors. **“Evolution of a distributed cooperation policy” is accurate; “cooperative coevolution of actor populations” is not established.** Shared-code deployment can still produce heterogeneous behavior because costs, neighborhoods, histories and private memories differ. [Potter and De Jong, 2000, *Cooperative Coevolution: An Architecture for Evolving Coadapted Subcomponents*, abstract and introduction](https://direct.mit.edu/evco/article/8/1/1/859/Cooperative-Coevolution-An-Architecture-for).

**Literature-supported interpretation.** In the broad artificial-life usage illustrated by Reynolds's flocking model, programmed individual behaviors can produce unscripted aggregate trajectories. Programming local rules therefore does not invalidate every emergence claim. Here the complete time path and realized distribution of ties are obtained through interacting offers, acceptances, unilateral deletions, changing costs and the shuffled activation order. [Reynolds, 1987, *Flocks, Herds, and Schools: A Distributed Behavioral Model*](https://www.red3d.com/cwr/papers/1987/SIGGRAPH87.pdf).

Nevertheless, there are different claims to distinguish:

| Claim | Status in this experiment |
|---|---|
| A network is generated by actor interactions rather than inserted as an adjacency matrix | Observed. |
| Realized delays, incomplete groups and exclusion need not be explicitly specified in the policy | Observed; an example is traced below. |
| The intended cohort partition or scheduled construction phase arises without a common convention | False for G5/G12/G26: the code explicitly computes and enforces it. |
| Macrobehavior satisfies a rigorous computational-irreducibility criterion | Not demonstrated. |
| An independently evolving society invents and maintains its own institutions | Not represented by the present deployment/evaluation design. |

Bedau's stricter weak-emergence formulation requires derivability from microdynamics and external conditions only through simulation. Running a simulation does not by itself prove that requirement. The safest language is **interaction-generated collective outcomes under an evolved common protocol**, with “emergent” used only after identifying the particular outcome and sense intended. No claim of strong emergence or irreducible downward causation is needed. [Bedau, 1997, *Weak Emergence*, pp. 3–5 of the author manuscript](https://people.reed.edu/~mab/publications/papers/weak-emergence.pdf).

**Rival explanation:** much of the apparently surprising macrostructure is the successful execution of an explicit clique-construction plan under a utility that strongly rewards triangles. The scientifically interesting residual is how local execution and heterogeneous incentives depart from that plan, rather than the mere presence of cliques.

## 2. The protocol supplies symmetry breaking and common timing

**Observed code.** G5 `main.java:163–190` maps public actor IDs into contiguous, nearly equal-sized cohorts. G12 `main.java:102–140` improves the choice of cohort count by comparing total utility across balanced partitions under the actor's own costs. G26 `main.java:169–221` retains that calculation and explicit interval membership. G26 `main.java:87–90` also supplies a public schedule:

```java
return observation.time() < 50.0
        || observation.time() >= 93.0
        || observation.triangleBenefit() < 0.4;
```

When this predicate is true, both action and acceptance use the original policy. Thus membership and timing are coordination devices available to everyone using the shared code. IDs do more than name actors: their numerical order determines allowable partners. The rule is generally not invariant to arbitrary relabeling of actors while preserving their physical histories and shocks. It would be misleading to describe membership as anonymous spontaneous symmetry breaking.

The partition objective also contains substantial structural knowledge. For an overlapping clique of degree `k`, the policy evaluates

`u(k) = (triangleBenefit − cost0 − cost1) k² + (2 + spilloverBenefit − triangleBenefit) k`.

This is an explicit local model of an ideal collective structure, calculated under a homogeneous-cost assumption. It uses no forbidden global cost observation, but it is richer than an actor simply following a scalar utility gradient. Under heterogeneous costs, actors compute different preferred partitions, which can make memberships incompatible.

**Interpretation:** the policy solves part of a coordination problem by supplying a common convention in advance. The remaining coordination problem is executing that convention with bilateral consent, differing incentives, bounded actions and an evolving graph. Random partner selection and shuffled activations add path variation; they are not the sole source of the group boundaries.

**Rival explanation:** sensitivity to arbitrary IDs or the known horizon, rather than a general capacity for decentralized adaptation, may explain performance on this fixed panel. This remains a hypothesis because no relabeling, timing or heterogeneous-adoption intervention has been run.

**A limit on the modularity interpretation.** Cohort logic permits several modules, but high clustering is not evidence of network modularity. At `d >= 1.2` on this grid, both coefficients of the ideal-clique utility polynomial are nonnegative even for the high-cost actors, and at least one is positive; the calculation favors one population-wide cohort. The review attributes 92.88% of aggregate utility gain to those incentive regimes. Thus the largest gains are not, on their own, evidence of discovery of modular organization. No modularity statistic or causal advantage of modules is established here. Likewise, the code demonstrates distributed coordination, but it does not measure collective intelligence across tasks, aggregate actors' independent estimates, or evolve specialized actor roles.

## 3. Memory is a rejection cache; topology also stores history

**Observed code.** G26 writes `memory.set(partner, 1.0)` following failed additions or rewires (`main.java:54–65`) and excludes that partner whenever `memory.get(partner) != 0.0` (`main.java:102–106,119–122`). There is no reset, decay or layer-specific index in this policy. One rejection therefore prevents that actor from initiating further construction offers to that partner on either layer. It does not prohibit the actor from accepting a later incoming offer, and the original-policy phase does not consult this custom blacklist. Engine memory remains actor-private and is never reset at the shock (`java-paper/paper/Memory.java:3–12`).

**Interpretation:** this supplies a mechanism for history dependence and economizes on futile proposals. It is not evidence of a learned reputation system, reciprocal altruism or enforcement of promises. A rejected offer can reflect incompatible cohort membership, rather than opportunistic defection. Moreover, neighborhoods, triangles and overlapping ties retain history even without custom memory: existing ties change the utility and feasibility of later actions.

**Untested hypothesis:** permanent rejection flags could also preserve obsolete refusals if partner incentives or the relevant coordination phase change. A rival is that refusals during this particular construction interval convey stable membership incompatibility, so the cache mostly eliminates waste. Demonstrating hysteresis would require a controlled reversal or state comparison; a single persistent network is insufficient.

## 4. Cross-layer reinforcement is partly in the payoff and partly in the rule

**Observed engine and policy.** The engine rewards overlapping ties directly (`PaperSimulation.java:61–67`) and updates overlap when an edge is added to or removed from the other layer (`:79–98`). G26 adds an explicit priority: first sample eligible cohort partners already connected on the other layer, then sample the rest (`main.java:92–132`). This is a distinct behavioral rule on top of the built-in multiplex payoff.

**Observed panel evidence.** G26's endpoint overlap is 0.935634 using the source convention, versus the original's 0.6541. But G12's overlap is higher, 0.959158, and its mean clustering is higher, 0.783073 versus G26's 0.750463. G26's zero-convention clustering falls partly alongside more low-degree/isolate observations; defined-only clustering is 0.879216 for G26 and 0.875826 for G12. Those denominators answer different questions. Source: `results/paper_trajectory_v2/report/generation-30-review/review.json`, `progress` records for G12/G26.

Consequently, “the later winner discovers progressively more overlap and clustering” is not supported. Nor does the payoff decomposition isolate the causal value of the overlap-first heuristic: strong triangle incentives account for most measured aggregate gain, and the earlier policy already constructs overlapping groups. The original Burghardt–Maoz model deliberately couples within-layer closure and cross-layer spillovers; the new result concerns a different policy operating within that utility structure. [Burghardt and Maoz, 2018, *Partial Shocks on Cooperative Multiplex Networks with Varying Degrees of Noise*](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-018-31960-y), also retained locally at `docs/paper_trajectory_v2/sources/article.txt`.

**Rival explanation:** overlap-first primarily changes the route and speed of construction. That interpretation is consistent with G26's mean cumulative recovery gain of +191.596181 over parent G20 while its terminal mean improves by only +0.001875. It is not a proven component-level causal effect because RNG consumption and all induced subsequent actions also change.

Degree 4, six incident triangles on each layer, and overlap 4 imply that the actor and its four neighbors form the same complete five-person clique on both layers. The structure is assembled by time 70 and still present at the time-93 snapshot. It then collapses after the policy returns to original immediate-improvement behavior. Therefore, this actor's terminal exclusion cannot be described simply as failure ever to construct the intended group.

The saved integer-time records establish event ordering, not the exact initiating deletion or the absence of intermediate rewires. Adjacency is saved only at 0, 50 and 100; no complete event trace is available for this case. A fall in triangles with unchanged degree does not, by itself, prove that all neighbors were unchanged.

**Interpretation and hypothesis:** a convention that enables investment need not maintain the structure after its withdrawal. The return to immediate improvement may expose a previously supported group to cascading externalities. The engine makes such externalities possible: deleting an edge removes incident triangle benefits from common neighbors as well as its two endpoints (`PaperSimulation.java:90–98`). The precise causal chain remains to be tested. A rival is that particular random opportunities at handoff, or prior differences among G5/G12/G26, explain the collapse without a general instability of completed groups.

This is an important distinction for self-organization: **formation, maintenance and recovery are different accomplishments.** Measuring only successful assembly or the population endpoint can obscure loss of a local structure.

## 6. Temporary sacrifice, terminal gains and exclusion are different measurements

The actor example also exposes a second distinction. G12 gives actor 34 a positive terminal effect of +0.4 relative to the original, but its accumulated end-of-round utility is 2,484.0 versus 2,693.6, a difference of **−209.6**. Its construction loss has not been repaid over the observed life even though its endpoint is better. G26's cumulative difference is **−262.4**. These are undiscounted sums of integer-time utilities, not continuous-time integrals, measured consumption, or utilities over an infinite future.

The full panel similarly separates the quantities:

| Program | Terminal mean | Actor-case terminal losses below −1e−9 | Isolates | Actor-case cumulative losses below −1e−9 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| G5 | 681.168854 | 31 | 3 | 1,533 |
| G12 | 681.194757 | 5 | 3 | 1,500 |
| G26 | 682.053021 | 287 | 105 | 1,531 |

Losses compare each policy with the original on the same 11,520 actor-case observations; the observations span related cases and are not 11,520 independent actors or histories. G26 improves terminal mean over G12 by **0.858264**, while its substantive terminal-loss count increases by 282 and its isolate count by 102. Different actors can enter the respective loss sets; count differences do not identify the same individuals switching status. G26 remains the winner under the frozen selection rule. These secondary results neither replace the winner nor establish a Pareto improvement.

**Interpretation:** “temporary individual sacrifice supports collective gains” is plausible at the policy level, but should not imply that everyone eventually recoups the sacrifice. Acceptance of immediate negative payoff is explicitly programmed during construction; whether that willingness is personally rewarded is an empirical distributional question. There is no biological reproduction or fitness accounting here from which to infer evolutionary altruism.

## 7. Two connections worth developing

**Programmable self-assembly.** Swarm robotics provides a useful comparison precisely because decentralized execution can coexist with an explicitly supplied target. Rubenstein, Cornejo and Nagpal show collective local interactions assembling programmed shapes. Here the target is a locally calculated cohort structure; Shinka proposes the protocol, and actors execute it. The analogy concerns architecture, not equivalent robustness or collective intelligence: this experiment has not tested heterogeneous policy adoption, identity changes or sensor/communication faults. [Rubenstein, Cornejo and Nagpal, 2014, *Programmable self-assembly in a thousand-robot swarm*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25124435/).

**Segregation without inferred intent.** Schelling's original work emphasizes that aggregate separation cannot straightforwardly reveal individual motives. This model makes that warning especially concrete: membership exclusion is explicit in the rule, whereas the high-cost distribution of eventual losses is an interaction outcome. It would be wrong to infer prejudices, national identities or “like-mindedness” from the numeric-ID groups. The useful question is which selection conventions and payoff differences generate exclusion, including exclusion not anticipated by the rule's ideal-clique calculation. [Schelling, 1971, *Dynamic Models of Segregation*, introduction](https://www.stat.berkeley.edu/~aldous/157/Papers/Schelling_Seg_Models.pdf).

## 8. Minimal future studies; none executed

1. **Does withdrawal of the construction convention cause the documented clique collapse?** After this campaign and separate authorization, run a diagnostic pair on the one declared actor-34 case: unchanged G26 versus G26 with only the return-at-93 condition disabled, retaining all other code and cases. Enable event recording for both and inspect the first causal divergence. This tests a specific suspected mechanism, not out-of-sample robustness; broaden to a preregistered history panel only if warranted. Compare actor endpoints, accumulated costs, triangle losses and consent/deletion events, not just population mean.

2. **Are arbitrary public labels essential, or simply interchangeable coordination labels?** On one homogeneous-cost and one partial-shock history, compare the unchanged policy with a fixed bijective remapping of public IDs, translating all neighbor/proposer/action IDs consistently while keeping physical actors and shock recipients fixed. This preserves a common convention but changes which physical actors share it. A further experiment with heterogeneous conventions would answer a different question and must not be conflated with relabeling. Record utility distributions and group compatibility. This is a separately declared interface treatment, not a modification to the running campaign.

3. **Which failures are caused by rejection memory rather than incompatible incentives or timing?** Use a separately authorized short diagnostic with only the permanent rejection filter disabled on a predeclared partial-shock case, preserving all other code and recording rejected/repeated offers. If behavior changes, distinguish saved proposal effort from restored opportunities. This does not yet test forgiveness, reciprocity or endogenous institutions. A test of public-clock dependence would instead shift the shock or horizon while holding code fixed; it addresses another mechanism and should not be bundled into this first diagnostic.

The present evidence supports an evolved common protocol that builds valuable networks in a particular payoff model and can impose both transitional and permanent losses. It does not establish anonymous self-organization, independent coevolution, universal resilience, or self-sustaining cooperative institutions. The late collapse example is especially useful because it turns these limits into a precise, testable scientific question rather than a generic disclaimer.
