# GLOBAL INSIGHTS SCRATCHPAD

The following are global insights about optimization approaches and their effectiveness:

## Successful Algorithmic Patterns
- **G21 remains the best program:** mean offline error **3.524052**, score **0.221041**. Its fixed radius multiplier **1.5**, four-particle default, and selective three-particle exception outperform every new candidate. The previous leading order remains **G21 > G14 > G23 > G19**; G25 follows with **3.586973 / 0.218009**.
- **Selective allocation retains an advantage over fixed-four relocation on this search suite.** G21’s mean error is **7.56% lower** than G27’s **3.812099 / 0.207810**. G25’s permissive gate and G28’s stricter gate also outperform G27, although neither matches G21. These results support the observed gated-policy pattern without establishing a causal benefit from state dependence.
- **The previous baseline comparisons remain unchanged:** G21 achieves **6.11% lower mean error than G6** and **15.28% lower than corrected baseline G0**. Shared upstream corrections remain baseline implementation work, not evolutionary improvements.
## Ineffective Approaches
- **Both loosening and tightening G21’s loss gate worsen aggregate performance.** G25 replaces `min(0.075, 0.10*coverage)` with `max(0.075, 0.10*coverage)`, increasing mean error **1.79%** to **3.586973**. G28 lowers the ceiling to **0.0625**, increasing error **3.68%** to **3.653841**, score **0.214876**. Thus, neither broader nor narrower eligibility improves the measured best.
- **Smooth radius adaptation does not recover the leading performance.** G26 fixes allocation at four and adjusts radius through saturating loss/spread signals. Its mean **3.808622**, score **0.207960**, is **8.08% worse in mean error than G21**, while only **0.09% better than G27**. This extends earlier evidence from G20/G22 that radius adaptation can alter the error distribution without improving the leading mean.
- **A smaller constant radius substantially worsens the fixed-four policy.** G29 changes G27’s multiplier from **1.5 to 1.4375**, raising mean error **7.17%**, from **3.812099 to 4.085441**, and lowering score to **0.196640**. Worst-case error rises **9.1811 → 11.8671**, and case_015 interval-end error rises **6.0212 → 10.3703**. These measurements show poorer tracking, but do not identify its underlying cause.
## Implementation Insights
- **G21 combines a spread boundary with a gradually increasing, capped loss allowance.** Three-particle relocation requires `diameter > 3R` and loss no greater than `min(0.075, 0.10*(1-(3R/diameter)**2))`. The allowance approaches zero near **3R** and reaches **7.5% at 6R**. G25 removes that near-boundary strictness; G28 restricts the allowance for broader swarms.
- **Successful adaptation remains rare, and frequency alone does not explain ranking.** G21 chooses three particles in **224/3,350 responses (6.69%)**, versus G25’s **236/3,329 (7.09%)** and G28’s **213/3,329 (6.40%)**. G21’s response-weighted allocated fraction is **78.66%**, close to fixed-four’s **80%**, despite its appreciably lower mean error.
- **Shared execution mechanics do not explain the performance differences.** Requested and allocated counts match, with no horizon truncation. Memory reevaluation and retained velocities are common to these programs. Non-relocated particles still receive ordinary PSO evaluations, so reduced allocation does not establish objective-query savings. G27’s saved metrics exactly reproduce G2’s, making it a repeated control result rather than a new improvement.
## Performance Analysis
- **Rounded scores obscure meaningful differences.** G21 and G25 both display **0.22**, but their exact scores are **0.221041** and **0.218009**. Among the new candidates, the order is **G25 > G28 > G26 > G27 > G29**; all remain below G21.
